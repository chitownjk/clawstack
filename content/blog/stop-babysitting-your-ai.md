You were supposed to be more productive with AI. Instead you have five browser tabs open, each with a different AI tool, and you are spending more time managing them than you would have spent doing the work yourself.

This is the babysitting trap, and almost everyone falls into it.

## The babysitting trap

The trap works like this. You discover an AI tool that can do something useful -- write emails, research topics, generate code. So you open it and start a conversation. It gives you a decent result, but needs refinement. You go back and forth a few times. Eventually you get something usable.

Then you need something else done, so you open another tool. Same process. Type, refine, copy the result somewhere useful. Meanwhile the first tool's context has expired.

By the end of the day, you have spent two hours interacting with AI tools to produce work that would have taken you three hours manually. You saved an hour, but it does not feel like a win because you spent those two hours doing something tedious: babysitting.

## Why this happens

AI tools are designed as chat partners, not as team members. A chat partner requires your active attention. You type, they respond, you type again. The interaction is synchronous -- you cannot walk away and let it work.

A team member is different. You give them a task, they work on it, they report back when done. The interaction is asynchronous. You can delegate and move on.

The babysitting trap exists because we are using team-member-level AI through a chat-partner-level interface. The AI is capable of working independently. The interface forces it to work interactively.

## How to break out

The fix is simple in principle: treat AI like a team member, not a chat partner.

In practice, this means changing three habits.

First, write tasks instead of starting conversations. "Research the top 5 pediatricians near Lincoln Park with ratings above 4.5 stars" is a task. "Hey, can you help me find a pediatrician?" is a conversation. Tasks are specific, delegatable, and completable. Conversations are open-ended and require your presence.

Second, use tools that support async work. If your AI tool requires you to keep a browser tab open for it to work, it is a chat tool. Look for tools that accept tasks, work on them in the background, and notify you when done.

Third, batch your reviews. Instead of checking each AI output as it arrives, review everything at once. This is the same principle behind checking email at set times instead of reactively -- it protects your focus.

## The delegation mindset

The people who get the most value from AI are not the ones who use it the most. They are the ones who interact with it the least. They delegate clearly, review efficiently, and redirect when needed. The AI does the work. They make the decisions.

This requires trust, which requires experience. Start by delegating low-stakes tasks. When the AI proves reliable, increase the scope. Over time, you develop a feel for what to delegate and what to do yourself.
